Which session of which course falls under each of the seven perspectives on statistics and machine learning.
Every session carries the stage it reaches — concepts, computation, derivation — together with the mathematics used in that session and the mathematics it connects to when taken further, kept in separate columns.

Seven perspectives

Methods divide for more than one reason. The question differs, certainly. But so do the assumptions placed on the data, the criteria for what counts as good, and the constraints on computation. Change any one of those and a different method suits the same prediction task.
The seven are not mutually exclusive; they look at different dimensions. The same equation often reads in several of them at once. Ridge regression, for instance, is a penalty in the geometric view, a MAP estimate under a Gaussian prior in the Bayesian view, and a control on complexity in learning theory. In the tables, a session that fits more than one perspective has the related perspectives named alongside it.

What this table can and cannot show

Can show: nothing reaches the derivation stage

Almost every session sits at concepts or at computation. Deriving the equations and proving the properties is not part of any course currently taught here. Whether some other part of the curriculum carries that role is worth checking.

Can show: the games perspective has few points of contact

Only session 13 of Speech Processing. The information cascade in Statistics II sits close to it, in that it is about group judgement once independence has broken.

Cannot show: that fewer rows means thinner coverage

Geometry has many rows partly because descriptive statistics, visualization and matrix computation are all gathered under that one perspective. The scope of the classification and the unit of aggregation are mixed together here, so the number of rows cannot tell you how thoroughly a topic is covered.

Cannot show: that something is missing because the mathematical prerequisites are missing

That cannot be separated from what happens to be assigned to this teacher, from the hours available, from the stated goals, or from how the tags were applied. This table alone does not settle the cause.

There are stages within reach even without the prerequisite mathematics

With finitely many hypotheses, a Bayesian update is arithmetic. Take the probability of heads to be either 1/4 or 3/4, with a prior of 1/2 on each, and suppose three independent tosses all come up heads: the posterior probability of 3/4 is (3/4)³ ÷ ((1/4)³ + (3/4)³) = 27/28. Normalizing takes addition; no multivariable integration is needed.
Information theory can be introduced the same way, through the entropy of finitely many outcomes, with logarithms and a weighted average. Generalization, too, asks for different prerequisites depending on whether a bound is being proved or the gap between training and unseen performance is being watched in an experiment. The question is not whether that mathematics has been studied, but which stage one chooses to work at.

The order the source document sets out, and what these courses touch

The order in which the source document places the mathematics, year by year. What the courses touch is noted alongside it. Whether that order is itself the right one is not examined here.

Year 1: sums of squares and inner products → normal equations and projection → extrema in one variable

Introduction to Data Use and Applied Information Literacy handle descriptive statistics and the idea of a sample, at the computation stage. Reading them as projections is not touched.

Year 2: QR, Cholesky, SVD → multivariable gradient and Hessian, IRLS

Statistics I goes as far as regression, AIC and PCA; Statistics II as far as probability, Bayes, and the doorway to causal inference. Computing the decompositions numerically is not covered.

Year 3: generalized eigenvalues, duality and KKT → kernels → alternating optimization in EM

Speech Processing handles EM, and the big-data courses handle PCA, k-means and PLS from the side of implementation. SVMs and kernel methods appear in no course here.

Year 4: Laplace approximation, variational inference, HMC → large-scale computation → backpropagation and SDEs

Session 8 of Speech Processing touches the training of large models. Bayesian computation, Gaussian processes, and the mathematics of generative models appear in no course here.
