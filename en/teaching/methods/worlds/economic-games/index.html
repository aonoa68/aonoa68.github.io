PERSPECTIVE 6 ｜ Games and adversaries

Economic Games

Design the payoff structure yourself, and measure cooperation as a decision the participant makes. The three games — dictator, trust and coordination — each measure a different quantity.
Following an experiment Onohara ran with 108 residents of a dialect boundary, this page sets out whether accent, taken as a “tag”, moves whom one pairs up with or how much one hands over, starting from what was used to measure it.

1What the method is for

Asking whether one picks a partner who speaks alike, as a choice with a payoff attached.

Cooperation among the unrelated can evolve when cooperators are able to find one another. One piece of apparatus for this is the “tag”: a strategy of directing cooperation towards partners whose observable mark resembles one's own (Riolo, Cohen & Axelrod 2001; Nettle & Dunbar 1997). The difficulty is that the strategy is open to invasion by those who can fake the tag. If merely imitating the mark draws cooperation out, the imitator profits and the strategy collapses. What the theory therefore requires is a tag that is hard to fake.

Accent has been treated as a leading candidate. The phonological patterns of a native variety are acquired early, produced largely automatically, and hard for an outsider to imitate convincingly (Cohen 2012). Running alongside is a lineage that reads dialectal difference as a marker of group membership or of shared norms (McElreath, Boyd & Richerson 2003).

The reason economic games are needed here is to measure choices rather than attitudes. Asking on a questionnaire which speaker one warms to, and putting a person in a situation with money at stake and having them decide which speaker to hand it to, measure different things. The experimenter designs the payoff structure and observes what people choose under it. This has become the standard frame for testing theories of the evolution of cooperation against evidence.

2Origin — the theory of tag-based cooperation, and the lineage of economic games

The theory side comes from evolutionary biology, the measurement side from experimental economics.

Tag-based cooperation — why “hard to fake” is the heart of it

Riolo, Cohen & Axelrod (2001) showed by simulation that cooperation can be sustained without reciprocity, through a strategy of giving to partners whose arbitrary mark is sufficiently similar. Nettle & Dunbar (1997) discuss the part social markers play in the evolution of reciprocal exchange. In either model there is an attendant fragility: the strategy is invaded once those who fake the mark appear. Hence the question of what marks are hard to fake.

Cohen (2012) took up accent as a candidate meeting this condition and examined it by drawing evidence together across fields. The grounds are three: it is acquired early, it resists conscious control, and the variation between groups is large enough.

Dialect as a marker — cooperation, or coordination?

McElreath, Boyd & Richerson (2003) showed that ethnic markers can evolve as signals of shared norms. Within that frame, the possibility arises that what a marker helps is not cooperation itself but coordination, the aligning of mutual expectations. The opposition between cooperation and coordination comes from here.

What has accumulated empirically — and where it is coarse

Infants prefer speakers of their native accent as partners for interaction (Kinzler, Dupoux & Spelke 2007). Adults allocate resources more generously to speakers of their own dialect, and the tendency is stronger where dialect diversity is locally salient (Cohen & Haun 2013). The largest test so far is Jensen et al. (2015), in which 4,603 Danish participants played hypothetical games with speakers of local and non-local accents. The result was that there was an effect in the coordination game and none in generosity or trust, from which the authors concluded that an ingroup accent facilitates coordination, not cooperation.

This lineage carries a shared coarseness, however: accent has been treated as a binary, local or not. The central quantitative prediction of tag models is that discrimination varies continuously with similarity, and a binary contrast cannot test it. This is the point Onohara's research takes up.

3Correspondence with Perspective 6, games and adversaries

Several parties each decide, while watching what the others do. Behaviour is read as that structure.

Perspective 6 of Reading the seven perspectives in equations is the view that reads learning and behaviour as the interaction of several parties in competition. Economic games are the original sense of that perspective. They take the framework of game theory itself — lay out a payoff matrix, have each player choose with an eye on their own payoff — and carry it into the laboratory.

Two kinds of “game” sit under the same perspective

This perspective already carries modelling cultural dynamics with a generative adversarial network. The two belong to the same perspective in that both involve the interaction of several parties, but the structure of interests they assume differs.

The objective function of a generative adversarial network, \(\min_G \max_D V(D,G)\), is zero-sum. The generator getting better means, directly, the discriminator being in trouble. The players are two networks, and the point of balance is the solution.

The three games treated here are all non-zero-sum. In the trust game the amount invested is tripled before it reaches the other party, so if trust holds, both sides gain. In the coordination game the matching of expectations itself produces the payoff. The dictator game is a one-way allocation and so has no strategic interaction, yet the structure of the choice — whom to hand it to — remains. The players are living people, and the solution is not a mathematical balance but the observed distribution of choices.

This also says that Perspective 6 handles more than the structure in which one side's gain is the other's trouble. Where the condition holds that there are several parties, and each one's choice bears on the others' outcomes, the cooperative side belongs to this perspective as much as the adversarial one.

4What the three games measure

Generosity, trust, the matching of expectations. All called “cooperation”, but distinct quantities.

GameStructureQuantity measured
Dictator gameOne side allocates a sum; the other only receives, and cannot refuseGenerosity in the pure sense. No need to reckon with the other's response
Trust gameThe amount invested is tripled before it reaches the other party, who returns whatever they chooseThe expectation that the other will reciprocate. The investment is a wager on their good faith
Coordination gameBoth gain if they make the same choice (here, meeting at the same restaurant)The matching of expectations. What one thinks the other will choose

The three designs are aligned with Jensen et al. (2015). The same participant plays all three against the same candidate partners.

Why all three are needed

Because the theoretical opposition maps onto them. If what a marker helps is coordination through shared norms (McElreath et al. 2003), the effect should appear in the coordination game and not in generosity or trust. If instead it helps cooperators assort with one another (Riolo et al. 2001; Cohen 2012), it should appear in the dictator and trust games as well. Setting the three side by side allows the two predictions to be compared within the same participants.

How this research answered the earlier conclusion

Jensen et al. (2015) measured generosity and trust as graded amounts given to a single partner, and found no effect on either. From this they concluded that what accent facilitates is coordination, not cooperation.

Onohara's research added one step to that way of measuring. It measured “whom to choose” and “how much to hand over” separately. The result was that the amounts showed no effect, as in the earlier study, while the choices showed strong discrimination in the dictator and trust games alike. Accent-based discrimination in cooperation exists, then, but it lodges in the stage of choosing a partner, not in the stake placed after the choice. It sat where a design that measures only amounts cannot see it.

This distinction coincides with where the biological-markets argument on the force partner choice carries for sustaining cooperation places it (Barclay 2013).

5The matched-guise technique, and quantifying tag distance

Hold everything but accent constant. Then turn the difference in accent into a continuous quantity.

The matched-guise technique — one speaker performs four accents

The technique was introduced by Lambert et al. (1960) for research on language attitudes. The same speaker reads the same passage in several varieties, and listeners evaluate them believing each to be a different person. Speaker, voice quality, vocabulary and speaking style are held constant, so differences in evaluation can be attributed to the performed variety alone.

In this experiment a single female speaker read a six-sentence self-introduction passage — a greeting and a short travel anecdote — in four accent systems: Tokyo-type at 25.2 s, Keihan (Kansai)-type at 22.9 s, the local Tarui type at 24.5 s, and, as an out-of-region control, Kagoshima two-pattern type at 24.3 s.

Tag distance — continuous, not binary

The central prediction of tag models is that discrimination varies with similarity, and testing it requires similarity to be a continuous quantity. The measurement pipeline of a companion study (S1) is therefore used to place the four stimuli in a continuous space of accent distance. The procedure matches speaker-normalized fundamental-frequency contours by constrained dynamic time warping.

The model of choice

The binary choice is handled by a mixed logistic model with a random intercept per participant. The effect of tag similarity and the effect of being the standard variety are put into the same equation and separated there.

THE TAG-SIMILARITY MODEL
\[ \operatorname{logit}\Pr(\text{choose A}) \;=\; \beta\,\Delta d \;+\; \gamma\,u^{\text{Tokyo}} \;+\; b_i, \qquad b_i \sim N(0,\sigma^2) \]

\(u^{\text{Tokyo}}\) … \(\mathbb{1}[A\text{ is Tokyo}] - \mathbb{1}[B\text{ is Tokyo}]\). An indicator that extracts the preference for the standard which acoustic distance cannot explain
\(b_i\) … the random intercept for participant \(i\). It takes in that the six trials × three games from one person are not independent

THE BRADLEY–TERRY UTILITY MODEL
\[ \operatorname{logit}\Pr(\text{choose A}) \;=\; u_A - u_B, \qquad u_{\text{Kagoshima}} \equiv 0 \]

Each accent is given a utility of “being chosen”, with the control stimulus fixed at the baseline 0. The ordering and the spacing of the varieties are estimated together from the table of pairings

Where the mixed-effects model sits

Because the same participant repeats six pairs × three games, the observations are nested. Why participants are placed as a random effect, and what properties that estimation has, are treated in linear mixed models and model selection by AIC. This research uses a binomial generalized linear mixed model via lme4, and tests whether random slopes are needed by likelihood-ratio test.

6How it is used in this research, and the results

108 residents of a dialect boundary. Three generations. Six pairs × three games.

  1. The area　The Kashiwabara and Imasu districts of Maibara City, Shiga Prefecture. This is the historical boundary at which the eastern (Tokyo-type) and western (Keihan-type) accent systems meet, and the local variety is the Tarui type, which carries properties of both. It suits the prediction that marker-based discrimination is sharpest where groups meet (McElreath et al. 2003).
  2. Participants　108 people, September to November 2018. Three cohorts: young (13–33 years, 38 people, 17 female, mean 18.5), middle (35–57 years, 35 people, 21 female, mean 47.9) and older (58–89 years, 35 people, 15 female, mean 69.5). The honorarium was a fixed ¥1,000, not contingent on decisions.
  3. Presentation　The four accents are presented in all six pairwise combinations. Two faces labelled “A” and “B” appear on screen, and clicking a mouth plays that voice. There is no limit on replays. The order of the pairs is randomized per participant.
  4. Perception questions　For each pair: which voice is closer to your own pronunciation, which is more different, and which do you not hear in this area.
  5. The three games　With a hypothetical stake of ¥10,000, the dictator, trust and coordination games are played. In each, the participant answers which of the two to take as a partner, together with an amount or a confidence rating.
  6. Egocentric distance　68 participants read the same passage themselves, and the distances between those recordings and the four stimuli were computed. This gives a fourth quantity: how close each stimulus is to the participant's own actual pronunciation.

Of 1,944 responses, two in the games and one in perception were unusable, leaving 1,942 choices for analysis.

Result 1 — the ordering was identical across the three games

The proportions of choice showed a transitive hierarchy, Tokyo > Tarui > Kansai > Kagoshima (control), and the same ordering in all three games. Bradley–Terry utilities (control = 0, pooled across the three games) were Tokyo 1.57 (SE .10), Tarui 1.01 (.09) and Kansai 0.39 (.09), all with \(p < .001\). By game: dictator 1.76 / 1.14 / 0.42, trust 1.23 / 0.64 / 0.21, coordination 1.45 / 1.19 / 0.56. The interaction between tag similarity and game was not significant (\(|z| \le 1.56\), \(p \ge .12\)).

Result 2 — a distance gradient, and a standard-language premium it cannot explain

Standardized tag similarity \(\Delta d\) predicted choice in the dictator game (\(\beta = 0.26\), \(p = .001\)) and the coordination game (\(\beta = 0.31\), \(p < .001\)); in the trust game the effect was weak and not significant (\(\beta = 0.09\), \(p = .28\)). Pooled across the three games, \(\beta = 0.27\) (\(p = .001\)). Allowing participant-specific slopes improved the fit strongly (likelihood-ratio test \(\chi^2(2) = 43.2\), \(p < .001\); random-slope SD 0.58 against a fixed effect of 0.31).

Yet the variety acoustically farthest from the local one, Tokyo (\(d = .383\)), was the most chosen overall. Entering an indicator for the standard alongside \(\Delta d\), both terms were strong (\(\Delta d\): \(\beta = 0.54\); standard-language premium: \(\beta = 1.41\); both \(p < .001\)). Among the non-standard varieties choice follows acoustic closeness, while the national standard carries a large surcharge that acoustic distance cannot explain.

Result 3 — perceived self-similarity dominates choice

The judgement of which voice was closer to one's own pronunciation agreed with the choice in 86% of dictator trials, 78% of trust and 79% of coordination. In a model with pair fixed effects and participant-specific random slopes, that judgement carried \(\beta = 4.05\) (SE .39), overwhelmingly; controlling for the accent-utility structure it remained at \(\beta = 2.60\) (SE .18), with the residual utilities shrinking to Tokyo 0.57, Tarui 0.31 and Kansai 0.13. Perception itself has the same two-layer structure as choice, with acoustic distance (\(\beta = 0.98\)) and the standard-language premium (\(\beta = 2.24\)) side by side.

Result 5 — the hierarchy is steepest in the older cohort and flattest in the young

Accent utilities differed by cohort (likelihood-ratio test \(\chi^2(6) = 17.7\), \(p = .007\)). The utility of the local Tarui variety rose monotonically with age: young 0.62, middle 1.10, older 1.36 (older versus young contrast \(b = 0.69\), \(p = .003\)). The utility of Tokyo was likewise elevated in the older cohort (\(b = 0.56\), \(p = .017\)). The youngest cohort has the flattest hierarchy and the weakest preference for the local variety.

EGOCENTRIC DISTANCE, AND ITS DECOMPOSITION
\[ \Delta d^{\text{ego}}_{ip} \;=\; \underbrace{\overline{\Delta d^{\text{ego}}_{\bullet p}}}_{\text{between pairs (the community's average position)}} \;+\; \underbrace{\Bigl(\Delta d^{\text{ego}}_{ip}-\overline{\Delta d^{\text{ego}}_{\bullet p}}\Bigr)}_{\text{within pairs (the individual's deviation)}} \]

\(\Delta d^{\text{ego}}_{ip} = d(\text{recording of participant } i,\,B) - d(\text{recording of participant } i,\,A)\). Without separating the two sources of variation, the structure of the community and the idiosyncrasy of the individual are confounded

Result 6 — production, perception and choice are at odds

Participants' own recordings were on average closest to the local Tarui stimulus (.350), then Kansai (.358), then Tokyo (.393), with the control farthest (.407). For 44 of 68 participants Tarui was nearest, and among the young 25 of 28. In the older cohort Kansai was nearest for many (10 of 16). Only six participants were nearest to Tokyo.

Despite that, egocentric distance did not predict behaviour. The effect of \(\Delta d^{\text{ego}}\) pooled across the three games was \(\beta = -0.01\) (\(p = .91\)), and neither the between-pair component (\(\beta = 0.04\), \(p = .50\)) nor the within-pair component (\(\beta = -0.04\), \(p = .49\)) was significant. Perception too tracked only the between-pair component (\(\beta = 0.29\), \(p = .005\)), not the individual deviation (\(\beta = -0.06\), \(p = .54\)).

That is, people who in fact speak closer to the local and Kansai varieties judge the Tokyo voice as “close to my own” and choose it most often. Further, an individual's departure from the community's average position bears on neither perception nor choice. The tag is being read at the grain of socially shared categories, not of individual phenotypes.

7Limits

The points the paper's Limitations section raises, and the constraints that come with the method itself.

1. The stakes are hypothetical

The ¥10,000 at stake is a hypothetical sum, and the honorarium is a fixed ¥1,000 not contingent on decisions. The paper itself raises this as a limitation. That behaviour may differ when a payoff genuinely rides on it is the standing point about the design of economic games, and it is no exception here. Jensen et al. (2015) also used a hypothetical design, so the conditions are matched for the purpose of comparison, but extrapolation to situations with real money at stake requires separate testing.

2. The matched-guise technique depends on the accuracy of the performance

Each accent system is represented by a single performed reading by one speaker, not by a native speaker's natural production of that variety. The authenticity of the performance may vary by variety. In fact the Kagoshima reading has a slightly higher median fundamental frequency than the other three (185 Hz against 161–165 Hz). The paper states that replication with several native speakers of each variety is desirable.

The paper goes further. The very fact that one speaker was able to perform all four systems well enough to drive strong behavioural discrimination is itself a reservation about the premise of a “hard-to-fake tag”. What this experiment can speak to, therefore, is how receivers read the accent tag, not the honesty of the signal in spontaneous production.

3. The dissociation between production, perception and choice is a finding, not a failure of measurement

Participants' actual pronunciation is close to the local and Kansai varieties, while their perception and choice face the standard. This is not an inconsistency of instruments but the content reported as the result. The paper reads it as self-perception having moved to the standard ahead of production, and argues it is a sign of language ideology operating inside the tag system.

What the variable “perceived self-similarity” is capturing, in that case, is a separate question. If it is not an acoustic matching against one's own pronunciation, then it is closer to a judgement of belonging to a shared category, “this is how we speak”. Since this variable is the strongest predictor of choice, establishing what it measures remains the next task. The paper raises the problem in the form of asking whether tag models need not only distance but also the value attached to a category.

4. How to place the standard-language premium is unresolved

How to treat, within tag theory, a preference for Tokyo that acoustic distance cannot explain (\(\beta = 1.41\) in choice, \(2.24\) in perception) is not settled. The paper states plainly that whether this is prestige as such or the result of the ingroup definition having been rewritten entirely towards the standard cannot be decided in this one community, because there prestige and orientation to the standard coincide.

The comparison the paper names is a core Kansai-speaking area such as Osaka, where a vital regional variety coexists with the same national standard. If Tokyo is still chosen over a robust local variety there, prestige is at work; if not, a categorical ingroup tag is. At present neither can be said.

5. Other constraints of design and measurement

The assignment of A and B to left and right was fixed within each pair and not counterbalanced (the paper states that position preferences were near .5 and that pair fixed effects did not alter the conclusions). The egocentric analysis is limited to the 68 participants whose own recordings were usable. Because individual deviations are measured with limited reliability, their null effects should be read as “not detected” rather than “shown absent”, as the paper itself cautions.

Perceived similarity and choice were also measured in the same session, so demand characteristics cannot be fully excluded. Models with pair fixed effects only mitigate the shared method variance.

As for the differences between cohorts, cross-sectional data cannot separate generational effects from lifespan effects. On either reading the implication stands that tag-based assortment is tracking a language change in progress, but the mechanism has not been identified.

Discrepancies between the published program and the paper

Reading the published voice_perception_experiment.py (239 lines) confirms that four voices and face images are presented in six combinations, that the order is randomized per participant (shuffle), that A is fixed on the left and B on the right, that clicking a mouth plays the voice with no limit on replays, and that clicks and timestamps are logged to list.txt. These agree with sections 2.2 and 2.4 of the paper.

The amounts shown on screen differ, however. The investment question in the published version reads “how much of ¥20,000 would you like to invest in which voice” and “how much of the ¥20,000 will you invest in that person”, with ¥20,000, whereas the paper gives a hypothetical stake of ¥10,000 for all three games. What the published version implements is also the single investment screen only, and does not contain the three games and the perception questions the paper describes. According to internal notes accompanying the paper, several versions were used in the experiment (test07, test08, test09), and which version the published file corresponds to cannot be determined from the materials to hand. Both statements are recorded here as they stand.

Which perspective this method belongs to

Economic games design the payoff structure and then observe what several parties choose under it. They carry the framework of game theory straight into the laboratory, and are the original sense of Perspective 6 (games and adversaries) in Reading the seven perspectives in equations.
Where the generative adversarial network under the same perspective handles a zero-sum contest, the three games here are non-zero-sum: if cooperation holds, both sides gain. What Perspective 6 handles in common is the condition that each party's choice bears on the others' outcomes, not whether their interests are opposed.
