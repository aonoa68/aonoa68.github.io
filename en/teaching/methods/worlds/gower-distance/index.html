Gower’s Distance

A procedure that builds a distance out of nominal-scale data, such as accent types. It counts nothing but whether two things match or fail to match, and takes the distance to be one minus the matching rate.
In chapter 6, case 4 of Onohara’s doctoral dissertation (FY2013), this procedure stands at the entrance to the numerical treatment of the accent systems of the former Tokuyama Village in Gifu Prefecture. What follows sets out what it does and does not measure, keeping to the figures printed in the dissertation.

1What the method is for

Composing accent types — data that can be neither added nor subtracted — into a single comparable index.

What is at hand is a description, locality by locality, of accent type. The types for e ‘handle’, ka ‘mosquito’ and chi ‘blood’ are written as sequences of high morae (H) and low morae (L): HHH~HHL at Hongō, HH~LH at Tonyū, LH in the Tokyo type, HHH in the Kyoto type. The question is a single one: how far apart are this locality and that one.

These descriptions cannot be compared as they stand. The number of morae differs from cell to cell, some cells carry two types written together with \(\sim\) between them, and a single locality is spread over fifteen cells. The dissertation states the reason as follows: “all the accent information of one locality has to be compared with all that of another, and for that it must all be composed into a single index on the same basis”. The policy it adopts is to replace the series of patterns built from the fifteen cells by a distance.

Why does the way of measuring distance have to be chosen at all? Because on a nominal scale — a scale on which neither order nor interval is defined — assigning numbers to categories gives those numbers no meaning. Suppose the types themselves were treated as categories: labelling \(\text{HHH}\) as 1, \(\text{HHL}\) as 2 and \(\text{LHH}\) as 3 does not make \(\text{LHH}\) three times \(\text{HHH}\), nor \(\text{HHL}\) something halfway between the two. Since a different assignment would yield a different distance, the resulting numbers would be an artefact of the coding rather than an output of the method. Gower’s distance looks only at whether two values agree, so the result does not depend on how the labels are attached.

For this data set, Euclidean distance would not change the ordering either

To be precise, however, the coding this study finally adopted is binary. The dissertation states explicitly that the H of a pattern was set to 1 and the L to 0, “converting them into binary variables”, before they were passed to daisy. For a variable with only two values the assignment of 1 and 0 is not arbitrary, and Euclidean distance is meaningful as well.

Indeed, writing \(m\) for the number of mismatches among the 52 positions, the Euclidean distance is \(\sqrt{m}\) and Gower’s distance is \(m/52\); both are increasing functions of \(m\), so the ordering of the localities comes out the same. Using Euclidean distance on this data would not change the conclusions.

What, then, is gained by using Gower’s distance? (1) The value is normalized to lie between 0 and 1, so that it can be read as “in what proportion of the positions do they disagree”. (2) The definition extends unchanged to three or more categories, to missing values, and to a mixture of quantitative and nominal variables. This study uses advantage (1); it does not use (2). The generality of a method and the part of it that actually does the work in a given application are two different things.

2Definition

Fix a degree of agreement for each variable, average them, and subtract from one.

The coefficient proposed by Gower (1971) was designed so that the variables may be of mixed types. For two objects \(i, j\) and \(p\) variables, one defines a per-variable similarity \(s_{ijk}\) together with a weight \(w_{ijk}\) recording whether that variable can be used in the comparison, and takes the weighted mean.

The general Gower coefficient, and the distance built from it
\[ S_{ij} \;=\; \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{p} w_{ijk}\, s_{ijk}}{\sum_{k=1}^{p} w_{ijk}}, \qquad d_{ij} \;=\; 1 - S_{ij} \]

\(s_{ijk}\) … the degree of agreement on variable \(k\). On a nominal scale, 1 for a match and 0 for a mismatch. For a quantitative variable, \(1 - |x_{ik}-x_{jk}|/R_k\), dividing by the range \(R_k\). An ordinal variable is turned into ranks and then handled in the same way
\(w_{ijk}\) … 0 if either value is missing, 1 if the comparison can be made. The denominator thus becomes “the number of variables actually compared”
\(d_{ij}\) … one minus the similarity. 0 when every variable matches, 1 when every variable differs

The general form is given here in its standard formulation. What is written in the dissertation is only the nominal-scale case set out below.

What this study uses is the case in which every variable is of the same type and nothing is missing. All the \(w_{ijk}\) are then 1, and the expression contracts as follows.

The form used in this study
\[ d(x, y) \;=\; 1 - \frac{1}{p}\sum_{k=1}^{p} \mathbf{1}\!\left[\, x_k = y_k \,\right] \;=\; 1 - \frac{\text{number of matches}}{\text{total number of elements}} \]

The dissertation defines it thus: “Gower’s Distance is a distance computed from the matching rate between combinations of nominal-scale data; it is the number of matching elements between the combination of one row and another, divided by the total number of elements in a row, subtracted from 1.”

For a binary variable, treating it as nominal and treating it as quantitative give the same value

Once H has been turned into 1 and L into 0, the degree of agreement as a nominal variable (1 for a match) and the degree of agreement as a quantitative variable with range \(R_k = 1\), namely \(1 - |x_{ik}-x_{jk}|/1\), coincide exactly. The same distance therefore results whether daisy regards a column as a factor or as numeric. Under the coding used in this study, the declared type of a column does not govern the result.

This special case is the complement of the simple matching coefficient, and equals the Hamming distance divided by the length. In other words, the computation actually carried out in this study makes no use of the general Gower form’s capacity to handle mixed types. What is used is only the match/mismatch branch of the general form.

3How the data were built

Expanding accent “variation” and “coexistence” into every combination of possible types.

The input is Table 6-1, “Accent types of nouns (+ particle), older generation”. For fifteen classes in all — 3 classes of monomoraic nouns, 5 of bimoraic nouns and 7 of trimoraic nouns — the type taken with the following case particle ga is written out in H and L. As the dissertation explains, this is the report by Sugito included in the paper of Nomura (1977), covering the accent systems of Hongō, Tonyū and Tsuka, to which the Tokyo and Kyoto types have been added from Kindaichi (2003), p. 71.

  1. Date of survey 1971 and 1976.
  2. Localities Of the eight settlements of Tokuyama Village, five: Hongō, Kadonyū, Hazehara, Tsuka and Tonyū. The dissertation states explicitly that Kadonyū and Hazehara do not appear in the table because their accent system is the same as that of Hongō.
  3. Informants Members of the older generation, at least two per settlement.
  4. Items analysed Nouns of one to three morae.

The difficulty here is that some cells of the table hold two types written together, as in “\(\text{LHL} \sim \text{HLL}\)”. The dissertation distinguishes two cases. What stands on either side of \(\sim\) is phonetic variation; what is written one above the other within the same cell is coexistence (in Sugito’s terminology, a case in which the pronunciation of that type is attested more robustly than mere variation).

Why the expansion is necessary

If a cell holding two types is kept as a single symbol, “\(\text{LHL} \sim \text{HLL}\)” becomes a category distinct both from “\(\text{LHL}\)” and from “\(\text{HLL}\)”. A nominal scale has no notion of partial agreement, so a locality that shows variation would count as a mismatch even against a locality that agrees exactly with one side of that variation. The wider a locality’s range of variation, the further it would come out from every other locality — a result determined by nothing but the way the description happens to be written.

The reason the dissertation gives lies one step before this. “This is because all the accent information of one locality has to be compared with all that of another, and for that it must all be composed into a single index on the same basis.” Reread a doubly written cell as “the set of types that may have been spoken at that locality”, and form every combination that picks one possible type per class for each mora count; a locality is then expressible in a single uniform format, not as one string but as a set of strings.

The reason for treating variation and coexistence alike, without distinguishing them, is stated as well: “whether the variation is between informants, or arises when a single informant is asked to pronounce the same word several times, or lies within the same word class for the same speaker, is unknown”, and the difference from what Sugito calls coexistence “is not clear”. The judgement is that the original description does not carry enough information to make the distinction, so the distinction is not made.

Number of combinations

For Hongō, the dissertation sets out the count concretely. In Table 6-1 the three classes of monomoraic nouns are \(\text{HHH} \sim \text{HHL}\) / \(\text{HHL}\) / \(\text{HHL} \sim \text{LHH}\), so the possible combinations come to the following 4 patterns.

HHH/HHL/HHL
HHH/HHL/LHH
HHL/HHL/HHL
HHL/HHL/LHH

In the same way the bimoraic nouns alone give 6 patterns and the trimoraic nouns alone 16, for \(4 \times 6 \times 16 = 384\) patterns. Tsuka gives 1024 patterns and Tonyū 4608. For Tsuka and Tonyū the dissertation does not report the breakdown by mora count.

Conversion into code

A single pattern with all fifteen classes strung together becomes a string of 52 characters made of H and L. The length 52 can be checked directly against Table 6-1: the monomoraic nouns give 3 classes × 3 morae, the bimoraic nouns 5 classes × 3 morae and the trimoraic nouns 7 classes × 4 morae, and \(9 + 15 + 28 = 52\).

On that basis the dissertation passed the data to daisy after setting the H of a pattern to 1 and the L to 0, “converting them into binary variables”. The unit of comparison is the mora, not the word (class). As section 6 shows, this choice of granularity governs the values themselves.

It should be added that the presentation slides reporting the same analysis at a talk and on a poster describe the original notation (\(\bullet\) high mora, \(\blacktriangledown\) lengthened second mora, \(\circ\) low mora, no mora) as having been replaced by the four values a / b / c / 0. The dissertation version is binary, H / L, with \(\blacktriangledown\) folded into H as a high mora. The values of the distance matrix printed in the two are the same.

4The computation itself

Line up the 52 positions and count how many agree. That is all.

The dissertation takes the first and second patterns of Hongō as its example. The two strings differ only in their last two characters.

The computation as printed in the dissertation
\[ \text{total number} = 52, \qquad \text{number of matches} = 50, \qquad d = 1-\frac{50}{52} = 0.038 \]

“There are 52 elements, of which only the last 2 elements differ. That is, the number of matches is 50, so Gower’s Distance takes the value 1-(50/52)＝0.038.” Since \(1 - 50/52 = 0.03846\ldots\), this agrees with the value 0.038 for Hongō 1–Hongō 2 in Table 6-2 below. There is no optimization and no iteration.

The most distant pair can be traced through the same formula

The largest value in Table 6-2 is 0.538, for Hongō 1–Tonyū 4608. Working back from the definition, the number of matches satisfying \(1 - m/52 = 0.538\) is \(m = 24\), so the two agree at 24 of the 52 positions and disagree at 28. Since \(1 - 24/52 = 0.53846\ldots\), this matches the printed value of 0.538.

Hongō 1 and Hongō 2 agree at 50 of 52 positions; Hongō 1 and Tonyū 4608 agree at only 24 of 52. The contrast between these two numbers is precisely the contrast between “variation within one settlement” and “a difference of system across settlements”.

The same example is also reported in the 2012 presentation slides, but the worked computation is written up differently there. Every figure and formula on this page follows the account in the doctoral dissertation (chapter 6, case 4).

5Running it in R

Pass metric="gower" to daisy in the cluster package. Nothing more.

The dissertation states that “Gower’s Distance was computed with the function daisy of the package cluster in the statistical software R”. The presentation slides for the same analysis give the actual script, in five lines. data is the table with all the patterns laid out one pattern per row and one mora per column, with H turned into 1 and L into 0. 

library(cluster)
dgower <- daisy(data,  metric="gower")
mgower=as.matrix(dgower)
diag(mgower) <- 0
write.csv(mgower,file="goweracc.csv")

What these five lines do

daisy is a function returning a distance object; given metric="gower", it computes the distance by the general form of section 2. It is by design the column types that determine the formula in daisy, but as section 2 showed, for a binary column either treatment yields the same value, so the declared type raises no issue here.

as.matrix turns the result into a symmetric matrix, assigning 0 to diag aligns the diagonal explicitly, and the matrix is written out to CSV.

The number of rows is 384 for Hongō plus 1024 for Tsuka plus 4608 for Tonyū, that is 6016. The output is therefore a 6016 × 6016 distance matrix.

6Results

Distances between patterns come first; averaging them by settlement gives the distances between settlements.

The Gower distances computed are distances between patterns, not between settlements. The dissertation therefore states that “the mean over all pairs of patterns within the settlements was taken as the linguistic distance between settlements”. This is an operation folding a 6016 × 6016 matrix into a 5 × 5 table. The dissertation calls the resulting value a pseudo linguistic distance.

The reading is straightforward. Patterns within the same settlement (0.038 for Hongō 1–Hongō 2, 0.077 for Tonyū 4607–Tonyū 4608) lie close together, and the distance grows across settlements (0.538 for Hongō 1–Tonyū 4608). But even within one settlement the value is not 0. Hongō 1–Hongō 384 is 0.173, which means that the two extremes of the 384 expanded patterns disagree at 9 of the 52 positions. The breadth of the variation appears directly as distance.

What it means for Tonyū to be distant

Among the settlements of the former Tokuyama Village, Tonyū alone has been held to carry otsushu (Tokyo-type) accent, Hongō and the others kōshu (Kyoto-type), and Tsuka an intermediate type. The dissertation sums up the figures of Table 6-4 as follows: “the linguistic distance between Hongō and the others (Hongō, Hazehara, Kadonyū) and Tsuka is 0.260, that between Hongō and Tonyū 0.475, and that between Tonyū and Tsuka 0.393.”

Hongō–Tonyū is the largest, and Tsuka falls between. The dissertation displays this table by metric MDS (cmdscale in R) in Figure 6-6, and states that “the accent of Tsuka is seen to lie between Hongō and the others on one side and Tonyū on the other”. The reading is that what had been called an intermediate type is shown actually to lie in the middle on a line.

This is, however, also a confirmation of a classification already known. It is not a result that reverses any ordering. The dissertation puts it modestly as well: “even after reducing the information to numbers, the differences in the accent situation of Tonyū, Tsuka and Hongō could be shown.”

Setting it against geographical distance

This linguistic distance is contrasted with geographical distance, for those pairs of points connected by a route. Geographical distance is defined by equation (1) below.

Equation (1) Distance between two points
\[ \sqrt{\bigl(\text{Euclidean distance between the points}(m)\bigr)^{2} + \bigl(\text{elevation difference}(m)\times 8.3\bigr)^{2}}\;\;(m) \]

The vertical distance is taken at 8.3 times the horizontal because, as the dissertation explains, “it is generally known that a person can walk 5 km in an hour, and that an hour is to be added for every 600 m of ascent” (cf. Langmuir 1984). This is not a straight-line distance but one brought closer to the effort of walking.

The formula in the slides carries no factor of 8.3 on the elevation difference, and can be read as having added the elevation difference as it stood. The linguistic distances are the same in both.

Whether equation (1) gives 682 m or 2516 m, the linguistic distance is the same 0.113. This follows directly from the fact that the linguistic distance distinguishes no more than three systems. The distance between two localities described by the same system is 0.113 rather than 0, and stays at that value however far apart they lie geographically. The dissertation states: “that Hazehara, Yamate, Hongō, Kami-Kaiden and Shimo-Kaiden show equal linguistic distances irrespective of their geographical distance is presumed to be due to something other than geographical factors, for example traffic volume; extracting that factor is left as a task for the future.”

The chapter goes on to compute betweenness centrality for a network of 44 points reconstructed from the circuit route of early-modern priests (the sna package in R, with weights from equation (1)), showing Tonyū at 84.000, Tsuka at 183.667 and Kami-Kaiden at 164.000, low by comparison with Hongō at 437.333 or Hazehara at 494.333. The correspondence is that the localities that are linguistically distant were also localities unlikely to lie on a route through the traffic network. This betweenness centrality is, however, an index on the side of the traffic network; it is not computed from Gower’s distance.

The same material coded at a different granularity

In the version of the same research presented in English, the unit of coding differs. One variable is not a mora but one of the fifteen classes, with the letters A, B, C… assigned to the types of each class. A pattern is then 15 characters long rather than 52. The worked example under this coding is “\(1-3/15 = 0.8\) for West1 and East4606”.

The distance matrix among the three lineages runs as follows: within West (kōshu) 0.2777778, within Middle (intermediate type) 0.3333333, within East (otsushu) 0.3794667, West–Middle 0.4444444, West–East 0.8462049, Middle–East 0.7077812. The concluding slide gives them rounded as 0.278 / 0.333 / 0.380 / 0.444 / 0.846 / 0.708.

The ordering agrees with the result at the mora level (West–East > Middle–East > West–Middle; at the mora level in the dissertation, 0.475 > 0.393 > 0.260). The absolute values, however, differ by close to a factor of two. At the level of classes, a class counts as a mismatch as soon as even one mora within it differs, so mismatches accumulate. This comparison bears directly on the limits set out in section 7.

7Limits

A value always comes out between 0 and 1. That it came out does not mean it means something.

All the expanded patterns are averaged with equal weight

384, 1024 and 4608 are counts of “possible combinations of types”; there is no evidence that each of them was actually spoken with the same frequency. That the nature of the variation is unknown is stated by the dissertation itself — whether it is variation between informants, variation across repeated utterances by one speaker, or variation within the same word class, cannot be determined. Averaging across settlements nonetheless assumes tacitly that all patterns are equally probable.

Moreover, the number of patterns differs greatly by settlement (384 : 1024 : 4608). The Hongō–Tonyū mean is a mean over \(384 \times 4608\) cells, the Hongō–Tsuka mean over \(384 \times 1024\) cells; the averaging is done differently for each pair. This procedure cannot separate how much of a distance value is a difference of language from how much is a difference in the amount of variation contained in the description.

The diagonal entries (0.113 for Hongō and the others, 0.240 for Tsuka, 0.240 for Tonyū) are means over pairs of patterns within a settlement, and express directly the extent of the variation in that settlement’s description. They stand in the same table on the same scale as the between-settlement values, but they measure something different. That the dissertation calls these figures a “pseudo linguistic distance” can be read as an expression carrying just such a reservation.

Binarizing into match or mismatch makes “near” and “far” types indistinguishable

Once reduced to 0/1 at the level of the mora, all that remains is how many morae disagree. \(\text{HHLL}\) against \(\text{HLLL}\) (differing only in the second mora) and \(\text{HHLL}\) against \(\text{HHHL}\) (differing only in the third) both count equally as “one mismatch”. Whether the accent nucleus has shifted towards the beginning or towards the end of the word — the direction of change — does not enter the distance. In the English version coded by class, the distinction is lost further still: both count as the single tally “that class does not match”.

The dissertation itself raises this as a task for the future. “Since the ordinary state of a dialect is not a simple opposition between the so-called otsushu (Tokyo type) and kōshu (Kyoto type) but one in which the characteristics of both are intermixed mora by mora and class by class, attending to those fine changes and to the direction of change will, it seems, be needed hereafter.” The very operation of composing everything into one distance flattens that intermixture.

Correlations among variables are ignored

The 52 positions are not independent. Adjacent morae of the same word depend strongly on one another phonologically, and words belonging to the same class move together by definition. Gower’s distance takes a simple, equally weighted mean over all variables, so the parts holding many variables that move together carry, in effect, the greater weight. The trimoraic nouns occupy 28 of the positions (7 classes × 4 morae), more than half of the 52. No grounds are given for regarding that as an appropriate weighting.

The general form of Gower (1971) carries a per-variable weight \(w_{ijk}\), but there is no statement that any weights were adjusted in this study. The reading is that the default equal weighting was used as it stood. This contrasts with the chi-square distance of correspondence analysis, which weights by the marginal frequencies: Gower’s distance contains no mechanism for saying which variables should count for more.

The values have no absolute meaning in themselves. They change with the design of the coding

For the same material and the same method, kōshu–otsushu comes out at 0.475 at the mora level of the dissertation (52 variables) and at 0.8462049 at the class level of the English presentation (15 variables). The ordering is preserved, but the numbers differ by close to a factor of two. No threshold reading such as “0.5 or more means a separate lineage” can therefore be attached to this distance.

What can be compared are only values computed under the same coding. Gower distances from other studies, or values from other distance measures, cannot be set directly alongside them.

The data are small in scale

The accent descriptions amount to no more than three systems (Hongō, Tsuka, Tonyū), based on Sugito’s report. Kadonyū and Hazehara are treated as sharing the system of Hongō, so three of the five settlements in Table 6-4 are entirely identical rows. Yamate, Kami-Kaiden and Shimo-Kaiden, which appear in Table 6-5, are all at 0.113 for the same reason. What looks like a 5×5 table carries only 3×3 independent pieces of information.

The informants are members of the older generation, at least two per settlement, and the survey dates are 1971 and 1976. The vocabulary used for comparison is the same across all the systems — 3 classes of monomoraic nouns, 5 of bimoraic nouns and 7 of trimoraic nouns, fifteen classes in all — so on that point the material is matched. How far these fifteen classes represent the accent system as a whole cannot, however, be judged from the sources. Nor can it be judged how far a description of variation obtained from about two speakers per settlement represents the language of that settlement.

It is a distance, not a statistical model

Gower’s distance carries no probability model. It brings with it no standard error, no confidence interval and no test. Whether the gap between 0.475 and 0.393 is a meaningful one, or one that would disappear under a slight change in the way the patterns are expanded, cannot be judged from this procedure alone.

This study seeks that judgement outside the distance rather than within it. The design is to set the figures against other kinds of information — the betweenness centrality of the traffic network reconstructed from the early-modern circuit route, marriage ties with Sakauchi Village, the accent systems described in earlier research — and to confirm that the isolation of Tonyū shows up simultaneously in several indices. No procedure for assessing the uncertainty of the distance itself is included.

Which perspective this method belongs to

Gower’s distance is not a procedure for setting up a model and estimating it. It is a procedure that constructs the metric space itself over nominal-scale data, and in that sense it comes before the analysis rather than being one. It belongs to Perspective 1 (geometry, least squares) in the seven perspectives in equations because it is at this stage that the geometry — what shall count as being close — is fixed.

In this chapter too, Gower’s distance stands at the entrance. The between-settlement distances of Table 6-4 go straight in as the input to the metric MDS (Figure 6-6), and become the edge weights of the network diagram (Figure 6-8, upper). Change the definition of the distance and everything downstream changes with it. Every method that takes a distance as input rides on the design of this stage without exception: that Neighbor-Net draws a branching structure from a distance matrix, and that metric MDS turns distances into coordinates, are the same dependence.

Not everything in this chapter rests on Gower’s distance, however. Betweenness centrality is an index on the side of the traffic network, with weights given by the geographical distance of equation (1). Correspondence analysis, likewise, is applied in the adjacent chapter 7 to data on population density and customs, and has an input separate from the accent distances. The two series are placed in parallel rather than in series, so as to see whether the same conclusion is reached by two different routes. A distance is not a universal entrance, but the entrance to what that distance can measure.
