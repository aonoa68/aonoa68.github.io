Logistic Reduction

The condition a dynamical analysis returns is an inequality in internal variables, and in that form it cannot be tested. This method substitutes the fixed-point relation into the support rule and reduces the condition to a single logistic regression coefficient.
It then sets out the two requirements that remain — that the estimate be made within judge, and that the bound is only necessary — through a counterexample and a measurement.

1What the method is for

Reducing a theoretical bifurcation condition to one quantity an experiment can measure.

The condition a dynamical analysis returns — \(\eta qk>4\), say (see Saddle-Node Bifurcation) — is an inequality in the model's internal variables. In that form it cannot be tested. Neither \(\eta\) (how heavily one reads others' judgements), nor \(q\) (how much of them one sees), nor \(k\) (how categorical one's own judgement is) is a quantity one can simply ask people about.

The method does one thing. It substitutes the fixed-point relation into the support rule and rewrites it so that it reads as the coefficients of a logistic regression. Two of the three internal variables then collapse into a single coefficient, and the bifurcation condition becomes an inequality in that coefficient. Whether the theory is testable at all turns on whether this reduction goes through.

Two requirements remain once it does: how the coefficient must be estimated for the structural value to come back (Section 4), and what follows if it exceeds the threshold (Section 5). The two limit the inference in opposite directions. Attend to only one and the measurability claim breaks.

2The reduction

Substituting turns the bifurcation condition into one regression coefficient.

Substituting the fixed-point relation \(r = a + \eta q\,\bar s\) into the support rule \(P(\text{support}) = \sigma(k(r-\theta))\), where \(a\) is the target's contribution and \(\bar s\) the fraction of others supporting them:

Reduced form
\[ P(\text{support}) = \sigma\big(k a + k\eta q\,\bar s - k\theta\big) \] \[ \mathrm{logit}\,P = \underbrace{k}_{\beta_a}\,a + \underbrace{k\eta q}_{\beta_s}\,\bar s - k\theta \]

\(\beta_a = k\) … the coefficient on the target's contribution
\(\beta_s = k\eta q\) … the coefficient on the support the target receives. This is the left-hand side of the bifurcation condition

The condition in testable form
\[ \boxed{\;\beta_s > 4\;} \]

\(\beta_s\) … the change in the log-odds of supporting someone as the observed support they receive goes from none to all
for scale … \(\beta_s=4\) corresponds to an odds ratio of about 55

What is measurable and what is not

The regression returns \(k\) and the product \(\eta q\), not \(\eta\) and \(q\) separately. For testing the condition this is sufficient — it is because the condition depends on the product alone that it collapses to a single bound rather than a pair of them. It is not sufficient for saying why a population sits above the threshold: a high \(\beta_s\) is consistent both with support decisions being highly visible and with standing being read sharply off them, and the regression cannot distinguish the two. Separating them needs a measurement of \(q\) that does not run through any judge's response — how far support decisions in fact circulate.

3Origins — non-collapsibility and the design effect

The method borrows two existing theories of discrepancy.

Logistic coefficients are not collapsible

In linear regression, dropping an irrelevant covariate leaves the slope unchanged. In logistic regression it does not. Pooling a population whose members differ in intercept (here \(\theta_j\)) or slope (\(k_j\)) returns a coefficient systematically smaller than the structural one each member is actually using. This is neither error nor confounding but a mathematical property of the non-linear link (Gail et al. 1984; Greenland, Robins & Pearl 1999) — the failure of the marginal and conditional coefficients to coincide, known in this literature as non-collapsibility.

The design effect and effective sample size

In survey sampling, \(n\) observations drawn in clusters do not carry the information of \(n\) independent ones. The loss is quantified by the design effect, and the discounted size is the effective sample size (Kish 1965). The concept belongs to the precision of estimates; here it is used instead to ask how many pieces of evidence a judge should count a crowd as. That is Section 6.

4Requirement 1: the estimate must be made within judge

A pooled coefficient comes back smaller than the structural one, so a null is uninformative.

This follows directly from non-collapsibility. A \(\beta_s\) estimated by pooling judges who differ in \(k\) or \(\theta\) is smaller than the value each judge is using. So \(\beta_s\) must be estimated within judge — manipulating \(\bar s\) within participants and recovering the coefficient as a random slope — and with described merit near the eligibility margin.

Saturated conditions do not bias the coefficient, but inflate its standard error

Vignettes describing targets far from the threshold push \(P\) towards 0 or 1. This does not bias the coefficient of a correctly specified model, but it removes most of the information available to estimate it, because the weight \(P(1-P)\) becomes small. The standard error inflates and the estimate fails to reach significance.

Put together, the conclusion is one sentence. A pooled cross-sectional \(\beta_s<4\) is not evidence against the model. It may only mean that the design did not meet the requirement. This is the same shape of problem as distinguishing absence of effect from failure to detect (Power Simulation), with the non-linear link as the cause.

5Requirement 2: a within-judge \(\beta_s>4\) is only necessary

What matters is not an individual's coefficient but the aggregate response of the judges.

Condition (5) is really a condition on the aggregate response of the judges whose decisions make up \(\bar s\).

Aggregate response and the general condition
\[ F(r)=N^{-1}\sum_j\sigma\big(k_j(r-\theta_j)\big), \qquad \eta q\,\max F' > 1 \]

when judges share a threshold … \(\max F' = \bar k/4\) and the bound is exact with \(\beta_s\) read as its mean across judges, which is what a random-slope model returns
when they do not … \(\max F'\le\bar k/4\), with equality only if every judge sits at their inflection point at once

A counterexample fixes the limit

Suppose half the judges respond as \(\sigma[5(s-0.25)]\) and half as \(\sigma[5(s-0.75)]\). Every judge has \(\beta_s=5\), above the threshold of 4. Yet the aggregate has \(\max F'=0.866<1\), so the map is a contraction, the fixed point is unique, and no fold occurs.

How much dispersion is tolerated was also measured: the fold survives a half-separation of thresholds up to about 0.15 and is gone by 0.20. Taking a measured \(\beta_s\) to the population therefore requires reconstructing \(F\) from the joint distribution of slopes and thresholds, which a random-slope model supplies only if it carries a random intercept as well.

This inequality was in conflict across sections

The same inequality \(\max F'\le\bar k/4\) had been written down from the start in the evolutionary section, as an upper bound for a heterogeneous population. But the section on measurability said that a within-judge \(\beta_s>4\) satisfied the condition. The same principle was in use in two places and only one of them recognised the limit. It was raised in the third pre-submission review; the counterexample was reproduced independently, and both passages were brought into line as a necessary condition.

6How many independent observations is a crowd worth?

Using the design effect as the weight a judge should give a crowd.

The \(m\) observed others are conditionally independent given \((a,\theta)\) only if each used nothing but their own private cue. In a running system they are all also watching each other, so their decisions carry a common component, and a judge who counts \(m\) observations as \(m\) pieces of evidence double-counts.

  1. Measure the correlation Holding the graph, the contributions and the private standards fixed and varying only the support draws, take the variance of \(\bar s_i\). It splits into a within-state binomial part, independent across judges, and a part driven by drift in the state itself, common to all of that target's judges. The second is the correlation a naive judge ignores.
  2. Convert it with the design effect The same calculation as in survey sampling turns it into \(m_{\text{eff}}\), the number of independent observations the crowd is worth.
  3. Put it inside the judge Raise the likelihood to the exponent \(m_{\text{eff}}/m\) (a tempered likelihood). This represents a judge who measures the correlation and discounts by exactly the right amount.
  4. Check that the approximation holds A variance-derived effective sample size entered as a likelihood exponent is not in general the same object as exact inference under the true joint distribution of the observations, since a correlation coefficient does not determine that distribution. So it was compared against a judge performing exact inference about \(m\) observers sharing a common shock \(\tau\).

The approximation does not change the fold verdict

Against the exact judge, the tempered judge returns the same fold verdict at every \(\tau\) tested, and the same \(\beta_s\) to within a factor of 1.00 to 1.06 throughout the range in which the fold occurs (\(\tau\le0.75\), \(\rho\le0.33\)). The two part company only well inside the no-fold region, where \(\beta_s\) is under 2 and the verdict is not in doubt. The integral over the common shock is computed by Gauss–Hermite quadrature on 41 nodes.

Given that, \(\beta_s\) is close to linear in \(m_{\text{eff}}\), and the fold appears between \(m_{\text{eff}}=1.25\) and \(1.30\). This is not an artefact of the logistic fit: iterating the true self-consistency map \(s=\sum_n\binom{m}{n}s^n(1-s)^{m-n}P(a,n/m)\) and counting its stable fixed points directly gives one at \(1.25\) and two at \(1.30\), agreeing with the fitted criterion \(\beta_s>4\) at every value tested.

How much should a judge credit the crowd with? That question is empirical, and the network model answers it.

Measuring below the fold is deliberate: inside the bistable regime replicates land in different basins and the correlation is near-total by construction, so calibrating a judge there and then using it to account for the fold would be circular.

The fold does not require correlation neglect

In a graded network the observed others are only weakly correlated — \(\rho\) between 0.02 and 0.12 — so the crowd genuinely is worth between five and nine independent observations. A judge that measures that correlation and discounts by exactly the right amount still produces \(\beta_s\) between 16 and 28, four to seven times the threshold. No appeal to naive herding (Eyster & Rabin 2010) or persuasion bias (DeMarzo et al. 2003) is needed. Correct inference suffices: the graded regime is unstable to accurate reasoning about it.

7Where the pressure stops — and why this is a sensitivity analysis

What is measured is the sign of a pressure, not a resting point.

Past the fold the same measurement returns \(\rho=0.983\) and \(m_{\text{eff}}=1.02\): every judge is moving with every other, and a crowd of any size is worth almost exactly one fresh private observation. A judge that discounts correctly there produces \(\beta_s=3.4\), just under the threshold.

The words have to be chosen carefully here. In the graded state, correct discounting warrants weighting the crowd heavily enough to destroy that state; in the folded state it does not, so the pressure that carried the system up is absent once it is there. But this does not mean that the folded state is a fixed point of correct reasoning.

The equation a fixed point would require
\[ H(C) = C \qquad\text{(not solved)} \]

\(H(\eta qk)\) … the coefficient a correctly discounting judge produces in a population whose parameter is \(\eta qk\)
what was measured … the single value \(H(10.80)=3.4\), and the sign of \(H(C)-4\)
what a fixed point requires … \(H(C)=C\), a different equation, which is not solved here

The pressure does not stop at the fold

Extending the measurement upwards gives \(m_{\text{eff}}\) = 2.57, 1.20, 1.05, 1.03 and 1.02 at \(\eta qk\) = 5.40, 7.20, 9.00, 9.90 and 10.80, against the 1.30 required: the crossing lies between 5.40 and 7.20. Correct discounting therefore continues to push the system up until \(\eta qk\) reaches roughly 6 to 7, some one and a half times the critical value, and only then stops. Accuracy does not deposit the system on the edge; it carries it past and stops pushing somewhat beyond, at the point where the correlation the arrangement has itself produced finally justifies ignoring the crowd.

This is the weakest of the paper's three lines of argument, and was downgraded to a sensitivity analysis

The effective number of independent observations was measured at a handful of settings on either side of the fold, and correct discounting was observed to push a graded system up and to stop pushing a folded one. \(H\) was measured at a set of points and the sign of \(H(C)-4\) read off; \(H(C)=C\) was not solved, and a system with endogenous \(\eta qk\) was not shown to converge anywhere at all. Doing either would require specifying how the judges' inference updates the parameters they generate, which is a different model.

The results of this section are therefore a sensitivity analysis — under the correlation structures examined, a judge discounting correlation correctly still produces a coefficient above the critical value below the fold, and below it above the fold — and not a statement about where such a system comes to rest. The manuscript says “stops pushing” rather than “comes to rest”. This was rewritten after review; the first version said “a fixed point of correct reasoning”.

8Numerical notes

A quantity that moves in steps is not reported as a point estimate.

The critical values are step edges

Support decisions are \(\{0,1\}\) and \(n_+\) takes only \(m+1\) values, so \(\beta_s\) moves in steps with \(m_{\text{eff}}\) and the critical values above are the edges of those steps. Across resolutions \((m,n_x)\) = \((10,41)\), \((10,81)\), \((20,81)\), \((30,101)\) we obtain \(m^\ast_{\text{eff}}\) = 1.296, 1.296, 1.152, 1.196 and \(\beta_s|_{m_{\text{eff}}=1}\) = 3.07, 3.37, 3.32, 3.30. Intervals are reported rather than point estimates. The region \(m_{\text{eff}}\le0.5\) is not resolved and is not used.

A coarse integration grid understates the coefficient (an error in the first pass)

Every value quoted in this section uses an integration grid whose spacing is at most \(\sigma_x/4\). The first pass used a fixed grid, which at \(\sigma_x=0.20\) is coarser than the cue itself and understates \(\beta_s\) there. The numbers were regenerated at controlled resolution.

Robustness of the measurement

The measured \(m_{\text{eff}}\) is unchanged by run length (5.86, 5.83, 5.83, 5.84 at 150, 300, 600 and 1200 steps) and by the number of replicates, and rises if the spread of contributions is widened (5.83, 6.46, 8.51 at \(a_{sd}=0.03\), \(0.10\), \(0.30\)), so the value quoted is the conservative one. Feeding the most conservative measured value, \(m_{\text{eff}}=4.82\), through the judge at every combination of \(\sigma_x\in\{0.35,0.75,1.50\}\) and \(s_\theta\in\{0.05,0.30,1.00\}\) gives \(\beta_s\) between 13.5 and 50.1, so the conclusion does not depend on matching the two models' parameters.

9A direct empirical test

Manipulate only the reported behaviour of third parties; read off one coefficient.

Because of the bound, the theory is testable by a single coefficient. In a vignette study in which the only manipulated quantity is the reported fraction of other people who have helped a described individual, a logistic regression of the participant's own willingness to help yields \(\beta_s\) directly — and comparably across populations, without any commitment to differences in psychology.

What the design must satisfy

Sections 4 and 5 become the requirements. Manipulate \(\bar s\) within participant, describe merit near the eligibility margin, and estimate the distribution of intercepts alongside the slopes so that \(F\) can be reconstructed. A measured \(\beta_s>4\) establishes the necessary condition, not the sufficient one.

Three further predictions

First, \(\eta q\) rises as merit becomes harder to observe while \(\beta_s\) does not: as \(\sigma_x\) goes from 0.20 to 2.00, \(\eta q\) rises from 5.4 to 38.3, a factor of seven, while \(\beta_s\) stays within 33.0 to 38.1. Populations judging difficulties with structural or hidden causes should therefore show a higher \(\eta q\) without necessarily showing a higher \(\beta_s\).

Second, the credulity required falls as the condition becomes more opaque, and past \(s_\theta\approx0.57\) the requirement falls below one (\(m^\ast_{\text{eff}}\) = 1.50, 1.30, 0.99, 0.59 at \(s_\theta\) = 0.05, 0.30, 0.57, 1.00). Making eligibility criteria explicit should reduce \(\beta_s\) even among judges who are not credulous at all. Raising \(s_\theta\) moves two things at once, so the roles were separated: holding the others' dispersion at 0.30 and raising only the judge's uncertainty takes \(\beta_s\) from 28.2 to 109.0, while holding the judge's uncertainty at 0.30 and raising only the others' dispersion takes it from 37.6 to 21.0. They pull in opposite directions, so the joint figure understates the opacity channel rather than being an artefact of it.

Third, the correlation among observed supporters is itself regime-dependent, so the same participants should weight the crowd less when the described community is one in which everyone visibly moves together, and more when its members appear to be deciding separately — a manipulation of perceived \(\rho\) rather than of anyone's disposition.

Which perspective the method belongs to

Perspective 2 evaluates how often a procedure is right when the sample is drawn again. What this method brings into that frame is two discrepancies. The non-collapsibility of logistic coefficients is the discrepancy that pooling introduces between the structural value and the returned one; the design effect is the discrepancy between correlated observations and independent ones. Both are statements about the properties of estimators, squarely within perspective 2.

What is unusual is that the second discrepancy is used not as the precision of an estimate but as the weight in an agent's belief. The design effect answers “how many independent observations is this sample worth?”; here it is made to answer “how many pieces of evidence should this crowd be counted as?” The same calculation is carried over from estimation theory into the description of an agent's reasoning inside the model. This method sits under perspective 2 (frequentist) in Read the eight perspectives in equations; the structural condition itself is under perspective 8.
