Perspective 6 ｜ Games and adversaries

Invasion and Fixation

That a state is possible and that a population is carried to it are separate claims. Evolutionary game theory addresses the second, turning parameters into strategies and running a process in which payoff differences become rates of increase.
It follows the model in which Onohara makes the way of judging itself the strategy, and sets out how trajectories, invasion fitness and fixation probability are used for different claims.

1What the method is for

Turning parameters into strategies, and asking whether a state is selected.

Dynamical analysis answers what happens if a condition holds (see Saddle-Node Bifurcation). But if the quantities in that condition are ways people behave, a question remains: is the population carried there? Left alone, does it approach that state or not?

Evolutionary game theory addresses this. Quantities that were parameters become strategies carried by individuals, and one writes down the process by which higher-paying strategies spread. The question is not optimisation but whether a minority holding a strategy grows among a majority. The framework was built in biology for kin selection and the evolution of cooperation; here the way of judging is the strategy.

There is one departure from the standard use. Normally the strategy is whether to cooperate. Here cooperating is not a strategy: the strategies are how heavily one reads others' judgements (\(\eta\)) and how categorical one makes one's own (\(k\)). Neither is a moral disposition; both are ways of reasoning, and their product is the quantity that appears in the bifurcation condition.

2Payoffs have exactly two components

Accuracy disciplines; conformity inflates. Both go into one equation.

Each agent \(j\) carries \((\eta_j,k_j)\), forms \(r_{ji}\) from a private cue and the support \(i\) currently receives, and supports with probability \(\sigma(k_j(r_{ji}-\theta))\). The payoff has exactly two terms.

Payoff
\[ \pi_j \;=\; \underbrace{S_{ji}\,(b\,a_i - c)}_{\text{epistemic}}\;-\;\underbrace{w\,\big|S_{ji}-\bar s_{-j,i}\big|}_{\text{second-order}} \]

epistemic term … whether support is reaching people worth supporting. With \(c = b\theta\), \(\theta\) is precisely the contribution at which supporting breaks even
second-order term … the cost of deviating from the group's verdict; \(w\) is its strength
\(S_{ji}\) … whether \(j\) supported \(i\) (0 or 1)
\(\bar s_{-j,i}\) … how much the others support \(i\)

The two terms pull in opposite directions

The first disciplines \(\eta\): weighting the crowd too heavily produces worse decisions and is punished. The second inflates it: a judge's own standing is set by the same machinery, so backing someone the others have written off marks the judge as a poor one, and withholding from someone everyone else supports marks them as uncharitable. Which way it goes is the question of this section.

The second term is a reduced form of an existing mechanism

What makes indirect reciprocity evolutionarily stable is the second-order requirement that one withhold from the unworthy or become unworthy oneself (Panchanathan & Boyd 2004). In that mechanism the cost is internal: the deviating judge loses standing and therefore loses support. The equation above debits the payoff directly. Being a reduced form is a possible weakness, so Section 7 closes the loop and reports what survives.

3Origins — evolutionary dynamics in finite populations

A stochastic process in a finite population, not a differential equation in an infinite one.

Pairwise Fermi imitation

Individual \(i\) imitates \(j\) with probability \(\sigma(\beta(\pi_j-\pi_i))\). The selection intensity \(\beta\) interpolates between neutral drift (\(\beta\to0\)) and always copying the higher payoff (\(\beta\to\infty\)). The form comes from the Fermi distribution in statistical physics and is the standard update rule for finite-population dynamics. Mutation adds Gaussian noise to \((\eta,k)\), bounded by \(\eta\in[0,6]\) and \(k\in[0.1,30]\).

In a finite population, “stable” is defined differently

An ESS in an infinite population is defined by negative invasion fitness. In a finite population drift lets a disadvantaged mutant reach fixation with some probability, so Nowak et al. (2004) replaced the criterion: a resident \(R\) resists a mutant \(M\) exactly when a single \(M\) mutant reaches fixation less often than a neutral one, that is when \(\rho_M<1/N\).

Fixation probability
\[ \rho_M=\Big[1+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\exp\Big(-\beta\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Delta\pi(j)\Big)\Big]^{-1} \]

\(\Delta\pi(j)\) … the difference in expected payoff between mutant and resident when \(j\) mutants are present
derivation … for the pairwise-comparison update the transition probabilities satisfy \(T^-(j)/T^+(j)=\exp(-\beta\Delta\pi(j))\), which gives this form
How to read it … the payoff difference is accumulated along the whole path, so small per-generation differences compound and the margins become large

4Three criteria, used for different claims

Trajectories depend on where you start, so stability cannot rest on them.

Three measurements are used side by side, and which claim rests on which is kept separate. They are ordered here not by strength but by what they depend on.

  1. Trajectories Run selection and mutation from an initial strategy and read \(\overline{k\eta}\) after 400 generations. Depends on the start. Without a neutral control (\(\beta=0\)) run from the same start, the effect of selection cannot be separated from the effect of drift.
  2. Sign of the invasion fitness Fix a resident and a minority mutant and read the sign of the mean payoff difference. Independent of the start. Judged by sign, not significance — a cell in which neither invasion is significant is a cell with low power, whereas evolutionary bistability requires both invasion fitnesses to be negative.
  3. Fixation probability Evaluate (7) over the whole range of \(\Delta\pi(j)\) and compare with \(1/N\). Independent of the start, and the standard definition for a stochastic process in a finite population.

The levels in the trajectories were an artefact of the start

Running the same sweep from other initial strategies gives, at \(w=0\), a selected \(\overline{k\eta}\) of 2.89 from \((\eta_0,k_0)=(0.2,4)\) against a neutral 1.53; 4.26 from \((1.0,4)\) against 3.97; 7.01 from \((3.0,4)\) against 11.15; 4.85 from \((0.2,10)\) against 3.88; and 29.63 from \((3.0,10)\) against 29.13. From \(\eta_0=3\) the selected value sits at or below its own neutral baseline: four hundred generations of drift-limited imitation do not carry a population back from a strategy it began at.

The boundary rule was checked too. Replacing clipping with reflection moves \(\overline{k\eta}\) from 2.89 to 3.03 at \(w=0\) and from 5.88 to 5.92 at \(w=0.5\). What survives every start and both boundary rules is the effect of \(w\) itself, raising \(\overline{k\eta}\) from 2.89 to 5.88, 4.26 to 8.90, 7.01 to 14.48 and 4.85 to 8.76. The trajectory tables are therefore presented as illustrations of the direction in which the second-order cost moves a population, and the stability claims were moved onto the invasion and fixation analyses.

Too little selection never reaches the optimum

At \(\beta=6\) the payoff differences are of order \(0.01\)–\(0.08\), so copying probabilities sit near \(0.515\) and the population is drift-limited rather than at its optimum. Under efficient selection (\(\beta=30\), \(w=0\)) the epistemic term by itself carries every replicate past the fold, to \(\overline{k\eta}=5.13\pm0.76\). The returns to accurate judgement do not stop short of the fold; they stop just past it. This agrees with the independently measured selection gradient: at fixed \(k=7\) the zero crossing is at \(\eta^\ast\approx0.62\), i.e. \(\overline{k\eta}^\ast\approx4.4\), eight per cent above the threshold of 4.0. The mutant offset used to estimate the gradient (0.25) straddles the bifurcation, so this locates the crossing only to within the grid spacing of 0.13 and should be read as “at the fold, slightly above” rather than as a point estimate.

5The expected-payoff process and the realised-payoff process are not the same

Two processes that go by the same name need not agree row by row.

The identity \(T^-/T^+=\exp(-\beta\Delta\pi)\) used to derive (7) holds for pairwise comparison on the expected payoffs of two strategies, which is the standard setting of the criterion and the one evaluated here. The trajectory runs instead let each imitation event compare the realised payoffs of two individuals.

Why they differ
\[ \mathbb{E}\big[\sigma(\beta D)\big] \;\neq\; \sigma\big(\beta\,\mathbb{E}[D]\big) \]

\(D\) … the realised payoff difference; equality fails whenever its variance is positive
consequence … individual variation in contribution adds payoff noise that weakens the bias in the realised process. The two processes need not agree row by row

Why this is stated explicitly

When the fixation table and the trajectory table disagree, a reader concludes that one of them is wrong. In fact they compute different quantities. The fixation table is the standard criterion applied to the standard process, not a calculation of what the trajectories would do if run to fixation. Alongside speed versus stability, this is the second reason the two need not agree row by row.

6Results

Past a threshold in the second-order cost the regime switches. No bistable band appears.

A prediction that failed: there was no bistable band

A band in which each regime resists the other — a range of \(w\) where either state can defend itself — was expected and was not found. At no \(w\) examined are both invasion fitnesses negative. The intermediate region at \(w=0.10\)–\(0.12\) is the opposite of bistability: it is a region in which neither strategy resists the other. The switch completes by \(w\approx0.14\).

This bears on what can be inferred from a comparison between populations, so it is stated in the paper. Among the two judging strategies compared, at the values of \(w\) examined, and holding the rest of the ecology fixed, no pair of regimes each of which resists the other was observed. That bounds the scope for path dependence in the judging rule rather than excluding it — a different pair of strategies, a mixed state, a value of \(w\) between those tested, or a long transient could each produce it. A difference in regime between two populations is therefore consistent with a difference in \(w\) and does not identify one.

What matters is that this is a claim about strategies, not about outcomes. The fold and support-matrix analyses show that the support matrix is bistable wherever \(\eta qk>4\), so identical individuals in identical populations can and do end up on different sides of it. Whatever the absence of a bistable band bounds, it is path dependence in the judging rule, not path dependence in who is included.

The fixation criterion returns the same conclusion

At \(N=150\), \(\beta=6\) the neutral value is \(1/N=6.7\times10^{-3}\). At \(w=0\) a lone gated mutant fixes with probability \(2.7\times10^{-7}\) and a lone graded mutant with \(1.8\times10^{-1}\), so the graded strategy resists; at \(w=0.60\) the figures are \(1.0\times10^{-1}\) and \(5.2\times10^{-22}\) and the verdict reverses. On both criteria the switch falls between 0.10 and 0.15. No row has both probabilities below \(1/N\); in every row exactly one of them falls below it. Repeating at \(\beta=30\), and evaluating \(\Delta\pi(j)\) at every \(j\) rather than interpolating from seventeen grid points, changes no verdict.

The cost acts on \(\eta\), not on \(k\)

The second term admits another reading. Since \(S_{ji}\) is binary and \(\bar s\) continuous, \(\mathbb{E}|S_{ji}-\bar s|=2p(1-p)\), which is largest at \(p=1/2\) and vanishes as \(p\) saturates. A cost of that shape could be paid down by polarising the support pattern — raising \(k\) — with no deference to anyone's verdict. It is not. Over \(w\) from 0 to 1.2, \(\eta\) rises from 0.415 to 1.355, a factor of 3.3, while \(k\) rises from 6.990 to 8.069, a factor of 1.15. The second-order cost operates on the weight placed on the community's verdict, which is the mechanism claimed for it.

Selection overshoots the collective optimum

Measuring only the epistemic component — whether support reaches people worth supporting — an \(\eta\) sweep at fixed \(k=7\) puts the optimum at the fold (\(0.0836\pm0.0096\) at \(k\eta=3.99\)) and declining beyond it: \(0.0723\) at \(k\eta=7.00\), \(0.0369\) at \(10.5\), and \(-0.0137\pm0.0118\) at \(21.0\), with accuracy falling from \(.987\) to \(.504\).

But the payoff is not a function of \(k\eta\). The support probability is \(\sigma\{k(x-\theta)+(k\eta)\bar s\}\), so \(k\) also sets the weight on private information. Holding \(k\eta=10.5\) and varying \(k\) over 3.5, 7, 14 and 21 moves the payoff over \(-0.0130\), \(0.0369\), \(0.0836\) and \(0.0869\) — the whole range of the table. The product is what the bifurcation condition depends on; the payoff is not. The right place to read it is therefore at the strategies that actually evolve. Measured at the six evolved pairs (where \(k\) rises from 6.99 to 8.07), the payoff is \(0.0829\), \(0.0840\), \(0.0836\), \(0.0789\), \(0.0657\) and \(0.0478\) (standard errors \(0.0092\)–\(0.0118\)). Along the path selection actually takes, the payoff is maximised at the pairs evolved under \(w=0.20\) and \(0.30\), just past the fold, and has fallen by about two fifths by the largest \(w\) examined, without reaching zero. What selection is shown to do is overshoot the optimum, not destroy the benefit.

7Making the reduced form internal

Stop debiting the payoff; close the loop with machinery the model already has.

Equation (6) debits a deviating judge's payoff directly, which is a reduced form: in the mechanism it stands for, the judge loses standing and therefore loses support. Since the model already contains the machinery that assigns standing, the loop can be closed instead of assumed.

Endogenous second-order cost
\[ a^{\text{eff}}_j = a_j - w\,d_j,\qquad d_j=\overline{\big|S_{jl}-\mathbb{1}[\bar s_{-j,l}>1/2]\big|\cdot\big|2\bar s_{-j,l}-1\big|} \]

How to read it … backing someone the community has clearly written off, or withholding from someone it clearly supports, counts against \(j\), while marginal cases count for little
the point … others then judge \(j\) on \(a^{\text{eff}}_j\) by the same rule applied to everyone. There is no payoff penalty anywhere

Three things carry over, two change

Carried over: the cost still acts on \(\eta\) rather than on \(k\) (over \(w\) from 0 to 3, \(\eta\) rises from 0.467 to 0.885 while \(k\) goes from 6.450 to 7.191); the regime still switches from the graded strategy resisting to the gated one resisting; and there is still no \(w\) at which both invasion fitnesses are negative, so the absence of a bistable band is not an artefact of the reduced form.

Changed: the threshold moves from \(w^\ast\approx0.13\) to \(w^\ast\approx0.6\)–\(0.8\), and the effect at comparable \(w\) is roughly a third the size. Debiting the payoff directly overstates what the reputational channel actually delivers, which is worth knowing and does not change what the section concludes. In the endogenous formulation, \(w=0.5\) gives both fixation probabilities above \(1/N\) (\(2.4\times10^{-2}\) and \(5.2\times10^{-2}\)) — the other way bistability can fail, with neither resisting.

8A second negative result worth recording

An effect not being visible is not the same as a mechanism not being there.

The dependence on \(v\) is real but masked by merit noise

The marginal cost of dissent should scale with \(v\), the value of being included. In the evolutionary sweep it does not: at \(w=1\), over \(v\in[0.1,2.0]\), \(\overline{k\eta}\) is 3.99, 3.58, 4.13, 5.03, 3.80 — flat to non-monotone and within the replicate spread.

The reason is not that the mechanism is absent. Holding \(a\) fixed as in the invasion runs, the standing component of the payoff difference between a gated mutant and its graded residents grows exactly linearly in \(v\), from \(7.3\times10^{-4}\) at \(v=0.1\) to \(1.45\times10^{-2}\) at \(v=2\). But so does the spread of payoff within a strategy, which comes almost entirely from \(a_j\) (its correlation with payoff among residents is 0.98–0.99) and is thirty times larger, 0.022 to 0.44 over the same range. Since \(a_j\) is redrawn each generation and carries no information about strategy, raising \(v\) adds noise to the selection signal as fast as it adds second-order signal.

This can be confirmed by intervention. With \(\sigma_a\) set to zero the per-encounter imitation bias grows with \(v\) as 0.010, 0.026, 0.051, 0.101, 0.194; at the paper's \(\sigma_a\) it is 0.002, 0.010, 0.021, 0.033, 0.042 and flattens by \(v\approx1\). At \(\sigma_a=0.15\) and \(0.05\) the trajectories become monotone in \(v\). The \(v\)-dependence is real but, at the paper's \(\sigma_a\), covered by merit noise. The instrument that sees it is the invasion analysis, which holds \(a\) fixed and compares matched groups, and that is the analysis the conclusion rests on.

In a heterogeneous population \(\overline{k\eta}>4\) is only necessary

If judges were homogeneous the population would fold at \(\overline{k\eta}>4\). With heterogeneous judges the self-consistent map has maximum slope \(N^{-1}\sum_j k_j\eta_j\,\sigma_j(1-\sigma_j)\le\overline{k\eta}/4\), with equality only when every judge sits at their inflection point at once. So \(\overline{k\eta}>4\) is necessary rather than sufficient, and the crossing shares in the tables should be read as upper bounds. The same inequality governs the measurability argument in Logistic Reduction.

Which perspective the method belongs to

Perspective 6 covers structures in which several agents act and each one's choice bears on the others' outcomes. Two methods already sit there. The objective of adversarial cultural dynamics, \(\min_G \max_D V(D,G)\), is zero-sum: the players are two networks and the solution is a balance point. Economic games are non-zero-sum: the players are people and the solution is an observed distribution of choices.

This method is a third kind. The player is a population and the solution is which strategy spreads over time. Nothing is solved for equilibrium and nothing is observed; a process is run in which payoff differences are converted into rates of increase. The answer is fixation in an evolutionary process, not equilibrium in a game. This method sits under perspective 6 (games and adversaries) in Read the eight perspectives in equations.
